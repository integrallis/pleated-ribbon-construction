\begin{abstract}
Probabilistic membership filters let storage systems skip reads for keys that are not present. Ribbon filters use much less memory than Bloom filters at comparable false-positive rates, but their higher construction cost limits deployment. We reduce that cost for homogeneous ribbon filters by grouping keys into approximate start-position windows with a counting-sort partition: one counting pass followed by a scatter pass. At 100--400 million keys, this recovers 98.3--98.4\% of the cache-miss reduction from a full sort at about one quarter of its reorder cost, and makes sequential construction 2.05--2.24$\times$ faster than the reference build. The gain comes from reducing misses in dependent row-reduction chains; batching does not help the tested Bloom construction or filter queries, whose misses are independent.

For linearly independent rows, we prove that banding produces the same solution under any insertion order. In measured cases that include dependent rows, reordered builds matched the arrival-order build's 64-bit fingerprint and had no false negatives. A slot-range parallel implementation takes 11.9 ns/key at 100 million keys with 16 threads. The method also transfers to RocksDB's shipped Standard128 ribbon builder, where filter construction is $1.8\times$ faster and measured false-positive rates match stock to six digits. In the evaluated compaction configuration, the total CPU reduction is within run-to-run variation. These results apply to the evaluated filters, parameters, and machines; they do not establish a general construction or deployment crossover with Bloom.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A membership filter is a small in-memory summary of a large key set. Ask it whether a key is present and it answers ``definitely not'' or ``probably yes.'' The first answer is the one worth having: a database that keeps a filter in front of each data file skips reading any file whose filter says no. Bloom filters~\citep{bloom1970space} have done this for five decades, at a cost of about 44\% more memory than the theoretical minimum for a given error rate. A newer generation -- xor, binary-fuse, and ribbon -- closes that gap to within 10\%, and to under 1\% in ribbon's bumped variants~\citep{dillinger2021ribbon,dillinger2022burr}.

There is a catch, and it is this paper's subject. The new filters are static: built once over a fixed key set, never updated. That suits write-optimized databases well enough, since their data files are immutable and background compaction rewrites them continuously. But every rewrite also rebuilds the file's filter, so build speed matters as much as query speed -- and building is exactly where the new filters lose. A ribbon filter costs about four times the CPU of a Bloom filter (RocksDB measures 140 vs.\ 32 ns per key~\citep{rocksdbribbonblog}), so production systems keep Bloom as the default and save the space-efficient kind for data they rarely rewrite. The people closest to these structures say so themselves. The parallel-BuRR authors report that ``a large part of the construction time is spent on parallel sorting''~\citep{becht2024parallelburr}, and the most recent proposals concede they build several-fold slower than the best existing builders~\citep{limasset2026zor}.

Which parts of that cost are real -- fixed by the hardware -- and which can be won back? Three experiments answer, and they collapse to one rule: optimization pays exactly where memory stalls form data-dependent chains. Independent stalls are already overlapped by out-of-order execution and software prefetch; dependent chains defeat both.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Ribbon construction gets dramatically cheaper (\S\ref{sec:ribbon}). Homogeneous ribbon banding at scale incurs 5.8 generic hardware cache misses per key in dependent row-reduction chains, and those chains are exactly what prefetch cannot hide. A counting-sort partition that groups keys into L2-sized start-windows (approximate order, nothing more) recovers 98.3--98.4\% of the miss reduction that the full start-position sort (SGAUSS~\citep{dietzfelbinger2019gauss}, BuRR~\citep{dillinger2022burr}) buys, at 5.5--6.1 ns/key where BuRR's own ips2ra sorter costs 23.9--28.1. End to end that makes construction 2.05--2.24$\times$ faster than the reference prefetch-pipelined build. Window size barely matters; totals stay within 13\% across $2^{13}$ through $2^{20}$ slots.
\item Banding is order-independent when the inserted rows are linearly independent (\S\ref{sec:parallel}). With first-coefficient-always-one pivoting, the proof gives the same solved filter under every insertion order in that case. Dependent rows can be dropped differently by different orders, so our implementation also compares each result's FNV-1a fingerprint with the arrival-order build and checks for false negatives. The fingerprints matched in all 54 measured runs. A slot-range parallel variant that defers boundary keys (at most 0.23\% of them) can therefore be checked against the sequential result with a checksum: banding is 4.25$\times$ faster at 8 threads and 7.56$\times$ at 16 against its own single-thread run (3.5$\times$ and 6.3$\times$ against the best sequential banding). The total is 11.9 ns/key at 100M keys.
\item The rule above came from failing twice, and the failures are load-bearing (\S\ref{sec:nulls}). We could not make batched probing win, and the reason turned out to matter more than the attempt. Miss-dominated point probes run into the per-core limit on outstanding misses; software prefetch was already at ~88\% of that ceiling when we measured it. Cross-key SIMD batching loses to plain per-key probing even with every address L1-resident. Bulk Bloom construction fell the same way, for the same underlying reason: its misses are independent, so the memory system hides them. Radix-partitioned construction in the style of \citet{schmidt2021partitioned} topped out at 1.45--1.71$\times$ over per-key insertion, a prefetch loop that RocksDB ships (but never evaluated) recovered most of that with zero transient memory, and our read-free register-aggregated apply (the idea we had the most hope for) came in 1.5$\times$ slower than partitioned RMW, because partitioning had already turned the target misses into L2 hits.
\item Along the way we ended up producing the first actual evaluation of the construction prefetch RocksDB ships behind a ``TODO: verify/validate'' comment (\S\ref{sec:prefetch}). It is worth 1.5--1.7$\times$ on unsorted banding. Partitioning mostly subsumes it.
\item The technique transfers to RocksDB's shipped Standard128 ribbon builder (\S\ref{sec:rocksdb}). One counting pass makes filter construction $1.8\times$ faster at 100M keys/filter and yields $25\times$ fewer banding cache misses at the same instruction count. RocksDB's tests pass and measured false-positive rates match stock to six digits; the E2 artifacts do not establish byte-for-byte identity. In the evaluated filter-favorable compaction configuration, the filter-build gain persisted, while the approximately 9\% reduction in total compaction CPU was within run-to-run variation.
\end{enumerate}

The primary x86 measurements use an i9-14900HX (AVX2, no AVX-512). Comparative numbers are produced by the field's reference benchmark suites, run unmodified at pinned versions: fastfilter\_cpp (its ribbon implementations are the reference code of ribbon's creator, Peter Dillinger) and BuRR. The only benchmark code we wrote for the homogeneous-kernel study is a thin driver that permutes the input order around the unmodified kernel. We also report ARM timing replications and a separate Graviton4 PMU follow-up (\S\ref{sec:arm}). Raw outputs and analyses are committed with the repository.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
\textbf{Ribbon banding.} A ribbon filter solves a banded GF(2) system. Each key hashes to a start column s and a w-bit coefficient row, and insertion XOR-reduces that row against whatever is already stored until an empty slot absorbs it. The lineage matters here. Dietzfelbinger and Walzer showed that sorting rows by start position makes banded elimination linear (SGAUSS). The ribbon paper's whole pitch was that you don't need the sort: insert on the fly, in arbitrary order. BuRR then brought the sort back (ips2ra, by bucket) because bumping needs it. Homogeneous ribbon, the variant we target, never fails construction and stores no per-key result rows.

\textbf{What the filter stores and how a query works.} A ribbon filter is an $m \times r$ bit matrix Z. Hashing maps each key k to three values: a start position s(k), a w-bit coefficient row c(k) whose first bit is always 1, and r result bits b(k). The filter represents k by satisfying one linear equation over GF(2): $c(k)\cdot Z = b(k)$, with c(k) occupying columns $[s(k), s(k){+}w)$. A query recomputes s, c, and b and answers ``present'' exactly when $c\cdot Z = b$. A key that was never inserted passes that test by chance with probability $2^{-r}$, which is the false-positive rate; at r = 7 that is just under 0.8\%.

\textbf{Why banding is local.} Construction solves the linear system incrementally: each insertion XOR-reduces its row against stored rows until an empty slot absorbs it. Because a key's coefficients span only w consecutive columns, all of the work for that key happens in a short run of slots beginning at s(k), and the expected run is short~\citep{dillinger2021ribbon}. Every technique in this paper rests on that fact: a key belongs to the neighborhood of its start position, so grouping keys by start region confines banding's memory traffic to one region at a time, and a thread that owns a range of slots can process every key whose run provably stays inside it.

\textbf{The homogeneous variant.} Homogeneous ribbon stores no per-key result rows during banding and never fails: a row that reduces to zero is dropped, trading a small false-positive elevation for guaranteed completion. Our configuration throughout is w = 64, r = 7, and $m \approx 1.09n$ slots: 7.63 bits per key at a measured 0.81\% false-positive rate.

\textbf{Binary fuse} construction partially sorts keys by segment explicitly to reduce cache misses~\citep{graf2022binaryfuse}; our Phase-0 characterization measures that fix directly (plain xor: 9.45 generic hardware cache misses/key; binary fuse: 1.08).

\section{Where layout tricks cannot work}\label{sec:nulls}
This section is about two optimizations that failed. Both got their best case: the batched probe was timed with every address already in cache, and the read-free builder was the design its own experiment was built around, each protocol fixed before any run. Both lost anyway, and that is the useful part: an optimization that cannot win even with the conditions chosen to favor it is telling you about the hardware, not the setup.

\subsection{Point probes are MLP-bound}
Experiment E0 compared probe strategies over identical split-block Bloom filters (Parquet geometry), 32KiB up to 512MiB, with the protocol frozen before any timing run. Our vertical, lane-structured batch probe does auto-vectorize from plain Rust. The packed-SIMD disassembly is committed. It still loses. At every size, even with every address forced L1-hot, per-key scalar probing wins (665 vs. 555 Mops/s), because a split-block check is already SIMD within a single key, and an AVX2 gather cannot beat eight independent scalar loads that the out-of-order window overlaps for free. The RAM-scale picture is starker: the identical instruction stream runs 5--6$\times$ faster when L1-hot. Probing is bound by memory-level parallelism. Prefetch was already at roughly 88\% of the single-core outstanding-miss ceiling. We measured it. There is no compute bottleneck left for a layout to remove.

\subsection{Blocked-Bloom construction is prefetch-hidable}
Experiment E1a measured five bulk builders at 1M to 429M keys, gated on bit-identical output (OR is commutative, so any deviation is a bug). Per-key insertion does degrade at scale (one random read-modify-write miss per key, 11.5 ns/key at 100M, IPC 0.48), so there was a real cost to attack. The attacks mostly failed. Radix-partitioned construction after \citet{schmidt2021partitioned} reached only 1.45--1.71$\times$. A software-pipelined prefetch insert, the shape RocksDB ships, hit 6.1--8.9 ns/key with zero transient memory and actually beat partitioning up to 100M keys. And the variant we designed this experiment around -- a read-free apply that partitions pre-merged (block, mask) pairs, OR-aggregates each block in registers, and issues exactly one store per block without ever reading filter memory -- came in 1.5$\times$ slower than partitioned RMW. Non-temporal stores changed nothing. In hindsight the accounting is obvious: partitioning had already turned the expensive misses into L2 hits, so the extra grouping pass was spending real time to save cheap loads. Bloom construction misses are independent, and the memory system hides independent misses better than we could.

\section{Partition-instead-of-sort for ribbon}\label{sec:ribbon}
\subsection{The target, measured}
We first measured where ribbon construction spends its time, using the reference benchmark
suite unmodified (three repetitions). At 100 million keys, homogeneous ribbon construction
costs 50.6 ns per key, and the hardware counters say why: the processor completes only 0.64
instructions per cycle and takes 5.81 generic hardware cache misses per key, which is to say it spends most of
its time waiting on memory. The reason is structural. Each key starts its elimination at an
effectively random position in a 1.3GB table, and every reduction step needs the result of the
previous XOR before it can even compute which slot it touches next. A prefetcher cannot run
ahead of a chain like that. This is exactly the opposite of Bloom construction, where each
key's memory accesses are independent and the hardware overlaps them for free.
\subsection{Design}
We only permute the input. One counting pass buckets keys by floor(s / $2^{16}$) (windows of $2^{16}$ slots, roughly 768KiB of banding state, under half an L2), recomputing the hash on the fly instead of materializing pairs; a second pass writes the keys back out in window order. The untouched reference kernel, prefetch pipeline and all, then consumes the permuted array. Everything downstream (banding, back-substitution, queries) is that reference code, not ours; the speedup is therefore attributable to reordering alone. Figure~\ref{fig:order} shows the access-pattern change this buys.

\section{Portability across architectures}\label{sec:arm}
We repeated the construction measurements on two ARM cores: an Ampere Altra (Neoverse-N1,
NEON) and a Google Axion (Neoverse-V2, SVE), each on eight-or-fewer cores in the cloud.
Table~\ref{tab:arm} gives the 100M-key wall-clock picture. Pleating wins on all three
architectures, and by a wider margin on the ARM cores: 2.05$\times$ over the reference build on
x86, 2.37$\times$ on N1, 2.72$\times$ on V2. This is what the mechanism predicts (the less
aggressively a core hides the dependent-chain misses, the more removing them helps), and the
reference banding cost tracks it (46, 114, 86 ns/key on x86/N1/V2). Parallel construction
scales near-linearly on the eight-core V2: banding speeds up 1.94/3.78/7.07$\times$ at
2/4/8 threads, for a 17.3\,ns/key total.

Correctness held across all three architectures. At each of the nine (size, seed) points, the
64-bit FNV-1a solution fingerprint matched across the Axion, x86, and N1 builds; every strategy
also matched the sequential build's fingerprint. These are empirical hash comparisons, not direct
bytewise comparisons. The order-independence proof establishes equality when rows are independent;
dependent-row cases are covered by the measured fingerprint and false-negative checks. There were
zero false negatives on every run.

A separate AWS Graviton4 run (Neoverse-V2, eight vCPUs) enabled user-space PMU access and measured
the banding-scoped \texttt{l2d\_cache\_lmiss\_rd} event. The median L2 read misses per key were
1.770 for the reference, 0.058 for partitioned construction, and 0.001 for LSD radix sort; the
partition recovers 96.8\% of the full sort's miss reduction. This is a mechanism check on a second
Neoverse-V2 host, separate from the Google Axion timing results above. The event is
microarchitecture-specific and its absolute counts are not directly comparable with the x86 event.

\section{Order-independence and parallel construction}\label{sec:parallel}
\newtheorem{proposition}{Proposition}
\begin{proposition}[Order-independence]\label{prop:order}
Let $E=\{(r_k,b_k)\}$ be equations over $\mathrm{GF}(2)^m$ whose rows are linearly
independent, each $r_k$ carrying a leading 1 at its start position. Then banding (store each
reduced row at the slot of its leading position) followed by back-substitution with all free
slots fixed to zero yields the same solution $Z$ under every insertion order of $E$.
\end{proposition}
\emph{Proof sketch.} The occupied-slot set equals the canonical pivot set of
$V=\mathrm{span}\{r_k\}$, which depends only on $V$; the stored system is row-equivalent to
$E$; and pinning the order-independent free coordinates to zero extends $E$ to a full-rank
system with a unique solution. (Full argument in the repository,
\texttt{docs/order-independence-proof.md}; with dependent rows the enforced subset may differ
by order, which the per-run fingerprint gate detects.)

Empirically, across every homogeneous sequential and parallel run we measured (sizes up to 400
million keys, six different insertion orders, thread counts up to 16), the finished filter's
64-bit FNV-1a fingerprint matched the arrival-order build. For dependent rows this hash comparison
is a measured gate, not a consequence of the proposition. When the independence condition holds,
reordering cannot change the output; the fingerprint also detects divergence in measured runs
that include dependent rows. Our parallel version assigns disjoint window ranges
(split at the same key count) to threads; keys starting in $2^{14}$ slots of a range boundary switch
to a sequential tail (0.015 - 0.226\% of keys), following parallel BuRR's boundary idea. Banding
scales 1.81/2.83/4.25/7.56$\times$ at 2/4/8/16 threads against its own T=1 (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling})
(3.5/6.3$\times$ at 8/16 threads against the best sequential banding; the parallel path carries
$\sim$20\% defer/copy overhead); what remains is the work we did not parallelize: the counting pass
(5.7 ns/key) and
back-substitution (3.8 ns/key at 16 threads) are still sequential, and together they now
account for most of the 11.9 ns/key total. Both are parallelizable (counting sort trivially; back-substitution as in parallel BuRR); we leave both to future work.

\section{Construction prefetch, evaluated}\label{sec:prefetch}
RocksDB ships a software-pipelined construction prefetch behind a comment reading ``TODO: verify/validate'' and the reference harness enables the same pipeline above 1500 slots. Our no-prefetch control isolates it: 1.5-1.7$\times$ on unsorted banding (76.9 $\to$ 46.1 ns/key at 100M). It attacks the start-locating miss but can't touch the dependent reduction chain, which is why partitioning dominates here while the similar prefetch strategy wins for Bloom construction (\S3).

\section{Pleating a production engine: RocksDB}\label{sec:rocksdb}
The measurements so far use a reference-kernel harness on homogeneous w=64 ribbon. The filter that
production actually ships is different: RocksDB's \texttt{Standard128RibbonBitsBuilder}, a
\emph{standard} (bumped-capable) ribbon at w=128~\citep{rocksdbribbonblog}. So we tested whether
pleating transfers to it in RocksDB's own build path. The patch is one
counting pass over the collected key hashes, inserted immediately before RocksDB's banding call
(\texttt{ResetAndFindSeedToSolve}); everything downstream is unmodified engine code. The order-
independence proof applies only to linearly independent rows (\S\ref{sec:parallel}), so output
equivalence here is an empirical question. RocksDB's own filter tests pass unchanged, and the
measured false-positive rate matches stock to six digits at every reported size. The committed E2
artifacts do not include a byte-for-byte comparison of stock and pleated outputs, so we do not claim
that this gate establishes bit identity. We
gate every measurement on a binary confirmed to contain the patch (object recompiled, binary newer
than source, marker string present) after an early build-failure artifact taught us not to trust a
stale binary.

\textbf{Construction microbenchmark} (\texttt{filter\_bench}, the engine's own tool; 10 bits/key).
Table~\ref{tab:rocksdb} reports total filter-construction cost, while Figure~\ref{fig:rocksdb}
plots the banding phase alone. At 100M keys per filter, pleating builds RocksDB's ribbon
$1.8\times$ faster overall. The banding cost is flat with filter size (\textasciitilde53 ns/key
from 1M to 100M keys) while stock banding degrades from 52 to
94 ns/key as the banding table outgrows cache. Below the cache crossover (a filter of $10^5$ keys
fits in cache) pleating is a few percent \emph{slower} (the counting pass costs something and
there is no locality to recover), exactly as the mechanism predicts.

\textbf{Why, measured.} The reason is the same dependent-miss chain, and we confirm it with hardware
counters scoped to RocksDB's banding call. At 100M keys pleating cuts banding cache-misses from
11.4 to 0.46 per key (a $25\times$ reduction) while the instruction count is unchanged (243 per
key, both). Same work, same arithmetic; the only thing that changed is that the working set now fits
in cache. Two things differ from the w=64 story and both follow from the mechanism. First, standard
w=128 spends \textasciitilde243 instructions per key against \textasciitilde98 for homogeneous
w=64 (128-bit \(\mathrm{GF}(2)\) reductions plus a per-seed rehash), so a larger share of each key's
time is compute that no layout change can remove, which is precisely why the \emph{time} speedup
is $1.8\times$ here versus $2.05\times$ for w=64, even though the miss reduction is comparable. The
memory win is real but diluted by more compute per key. Second, RocksDB's shipped construction
prefetch (\S\ref{sec:prefetch}) attacks the same start-locating miss; pleating subsumes it (pleated
construction is unchanged whether the prefetch is on or off), consistent with the homogeneous
finding that partitioning makes the prefetch redundant.

\textbf{End-to-end during compaction} (\texttt{db\_bench}: \texttt{fillrandom} 30M keys then full
compaction, 8-byte values and 256\,MB target SSTs so filters are a meaningful share of compaction;
three reps). Here the honest accounting matters more than a headline. Filter construction runs during
background compaction, so it does not sit on the write path, and write throughput is unchanged. The
robust, reproducible result is that the $\sim$2$\times$ filter-build speedup survives inside real
compaction: banding drops $77.6\pm1.5 \to 44.2\pm6.1$ ns/key (means of 3; the isolated,
uninstrumented build shows the fuller $2.0\times$, compressed here by the statistics
instrumentation). Filter banding is $\sim$23\% of compaction CPU in this configuration (1.83\,s of
7.9\,s), so halving it reclaims a real slice. We are deliberately careful about the whole-compaction
number: total compaction CPU falls $\sim$9\% ($7.9\pm0.8 \to 7.2\pm0.4$\,s), but that difference is
of the same order as the run-to-run variance of compaction itself (which is nondeterministic), so we
report it as directional, not tightly bounded. The effect also grows with per-SST filter size and
vanishes below the cache crossover, so its magnitude is a function of SST sizing; this is the
filter-favorable end of that range. In this RocksDB configuration, pleating reduces the measured
cost of filter construction, with RocksDB's tests passing and measured false-positive rates
unchanged to six digits. The whole-compaction change remains directional at the measured sample
size.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
Sorted banded elimination: \citet{dietzfelbinger2019gauss}, BuRR~\citep{dillinger2022burr}, parallel BuRR~\citep{becht2024parallelburr}. Arbitrary-order banding and production engineering: ribbon~\citep{dillinger2021ribbon}, RocksDB~\citep{rocksdbribbonblog}. Cache-conscious filter design: buffered Bloom filters~\citep{canim2010buffered}, radix-partitioned filters~\citep{schmidt2021partitioned}, binary-fuse segment sort~\citep{graf2022binaryfuse}, xor-filter buffered scatter~\citep{graf2020xor}; the generic two-phase partition-then-accumulate pattern appears as propagation blocking~\citep{beamer2017propagation} with write-combining from~\citet{wassenberg2011radix}. SIMD probe-side layout: \citet{polychroniou2014vectorized}, \citet{lang2019performance}, split-block Bloom~\citep{apache2021sbbf}. Our delta to BuRR is precise: BuRR sorts fully (ips2ra) to enable threshold-based bumping and tighter space; we show that for homogeneous ribbon a counting-pass partition captures 98\% of the sort's locality at 4$\times$ less reorder cost, with output neutrality making the transformation and its parallelization verifiable.

\section{Limitations and threats to validity}\label{sec:limits}
We characterize the miss mechanism primarily with x86 hardware counters; a separate AWS Graviton4 run also measures the Neoverse-V2 L2 read-miss event (\S\ref{sec:arm}). Since the ARM event differs from the x86 generic cache-miss event, we compare reductions within each machine and do not pool absolute counts. The original ARM timing replications confirm wall-clock speedups and matching output fingerprints. We accelerate the \emph{homogeneous} ribbon ($\sim$9.5\% space overhead at r=7) in comparison to BuRR (which is sub-1\% overhead); BuRR is a mechanistic anchor, not a like-for-like baseline. Sort baselines use BuRR's own sequential ips2ra, but not its parallel form. Our parallel comparison is limited to banding, and its headline ratios are against the parallel path's own single-thread run. The order-independence proposition is proved for linearly independent rows; dependent-row behavior is assessed empirically by comparing fingerprints with the arrival-order build and checking for false negatives. The RocksDB construction gain is measured on the production w=128 kernel; E2 records matching false-positive rates to six digits and passing RocksDB tests, but does not contain a byte-for-byte output comparison. Its whole-compaction result comes from one filter-favorable configuration: the measured reduction is directional, falls within run-to-run variation, and depends on SST sizing.

\section{Conclusion}
In the measured configurations, a counting-sort partition recovers 98\% of the full sort's cache-miss reduction for homogeneous ribbon construction at about a quarter of the reorder cost. For linearly independent rows, the order-independence proof supports reordering; the measured dependent-row cases are checked against the arrival-order fingerprint and for false negatives. The method also improves construction of RocksDB's shipped w=128 ribbon builder by $1.8\times$ at 100M keys/filter. RocksDB's tests pass and measured false-positive rates match stock to six digits, but the available E2 artifacts do not establish byte-for-byte identity. The measured whole-compaction change is directional and configuration-dependent. These results locate a construction opportunity in the evaluated filters and machines; they do not establish that ribbon universally matches Bloom's deployment cost.
