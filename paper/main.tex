\begin{abstract}
Probabilistic membership filters — Bloom filters and their modern successors — answer a single question: is this key possibly in this set? Storage systems keep one in front of nearly every file on disk so that a lookup that would find nothing can skip the disk read entirely. Newer filter designs, such as ribbon and binary fuse filters, deliver the same accuracy as Bloom filters in far less memory, within a few percent of the theoretical minimum. Yet they have not displaced Bloom filters in practice, and the reason is construction speed: these filters are immutable, storage engines rebuild them constantly as data is compacted, and building a ribbon filter costs roughly four times the CPU of building a Bloom filter.

This paper closes most of that gap for homogeneous ribbon filters. A single counting pass that groups keys by the region of the filter they touch — a weaker and roughly four-times-cheaper form of the full sort used in prior work — makes sequential construction 2.05--2.24$\times$ faster at 100–400 million keys. The method is safe and cheap to trust because of a property we prove and then verify in every run: the finished filter is bit-for-bit identical regardless of insertion order, so any reordering, including a parallel one, can confirm its own correctness by comparing a single checksum against the sequential build. Behind the speedup sits one mechanical rule about modern processors, established by two negative results: batching and layout tricks do nothing for filter queries or for Bloom construction, whose memory stalls are independent and already overlapped by out-of-order hardware — but ribbon construction stalls form dependent chains, each elimination step waiting on the one before, and dependent chains are the one memory cost that hardware cannot hide and reordering can remove. The upshot, with 16 threads, is a ribbon filter built as fast as a single thread inserts into a Bloom filter — the fastest construction we know of for this filter on the hardware tested, and the end of the main reason to keep choosing Bloom: the filter that needs a quarter less memory at the same accuracy no longer costs more to build.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A membership filter is a small in-memory summary of a large set of keys. Ask it whether a key is present and it answers either ``definitely not'' or ``probably yes,'' and the first answer is the valuable one: a database that keeps a filter in front of each data file can skip reading any file whose filter says no. Bloom filters~\citep{bloom1970space} have played this role for five decades, but they spend about 44\% more memory than the theoretical minimum for a given error rate. A newer generation of filters — xor, binary fuse, and ribbon filters — closes that gap to within 10\% and, in ribbon's bumped variants, under 1\%~\citep{dillinger2021ribbon,dillinger2022burr}.

There is a catch, and it is the subject of this paper. The new filters are static: they are built once over a fixed key set and never updated. That fits how write-optimized databases actually work — data files are immutable, and background compaction continuously rewrites them — but it means every rewrite also rebuilds the file's filter, so construction speed matters as much as query speed. And construction is where the new filters lose: building a ribbon filter costs roughly four times the CPU of building a Bloom filter (RocksDB measures 140 vs.\ 32 ns per key~\citep{rocksdbribbonblog}), which is why production systems still default to Bloom filters and reserve the space-efficient kind for data they rarely rewrite. The people closest to these structures say as much: the parallel-BuRR authors note that ``a large part of the construction time is spent on parallel sorting''~\citep{becht2024parallelburr}, and the most recent proposals concede they build several-fold slower than the best existing builders~\citep{limasset2026zor}.

This paper asks which parts of that cost are real — pinned to the hardware — and which parts can be won back. We answer with three experiments, and the answer reduces to one mechanical rule: optimization pays exactly where memory stalls form data-dependent chains, because independent stalls are already overlapped by out-of-order hardware and software prefetch, while dependent chains defeat both.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Ribbon construction gets dramatically cheaper (§4). Homogeneous ribbon banding at scale burns 5.8 DRAM misses per key in dependent row-reduction chains, and those chains are exactly what prefetch cannot hide. A single counting pass that groups keys into L2-sized start-windows — approximate order, nothing more — recovers 98.3--98.4\% of the miss reduction that the full start-position sort (SGAUSS~\citep{dietzfelbinger2019gauss}, BuRR~\citep{dillinger2022burr}) buys, at 5.5--6.1 ns/key where BuRR's own ips2ra sorter costs 23.9--28.1. End to end that makes construction 2.05--2.24$\times$ faster than the reference prefetch-pipelined build. Window size barely matters; totals stay within 13\% across $2^{13}$ through $2^{20}$ slots.
\item Banding turns out to be order-independent, and that observation carries real weight (§5). With first-coefficient-always-one pivoting, the solved filter is bit-identical under any insertion order — we prove this for linearly independent rows and saw it hold in 54 of 54 runs. So reordering is output-neutral by construction, and a slot-range parallel variant that defers boundary keys (at most 0.23\% of them) can verify itself with a checksum: 4.25$\times$ faster banding at 8 threads, 7.56$\times$ at 16, against its own single-thread run (3.5$\times$ and 6.3$\times$ against the best sequential banding). The total lands at 11.9 ns/key at 100M keys.
\item The rule above came from failing twice, and the failures are load-bearing (\S\ref{sec:nulls}). We could not make batched probing win, and the reason turned out to matter more than the attempt. Miss-dominated point probes run into the per-core limit on outstanding misses; software prefetch was already at ~88\% of that ceiling when we measured it. Cross-key SIMD batching loses to plain per-key probing even with every address L1-resident. Bulk Bloom construction fell the same way, for the same underlying reason: its misses are independent, so the memory system hides them. Radix-partitioned construction in the style of \citet{schmidt2021partitioned} topped out at 1.45--1.71$\times$ over per-key insertion, a prefetch loop that RocksDB ships (but never evaluated) recovered most of that with zero transient memory, and our read-free register-aggregated apply — the idea we had the most hope for — came in 1.5$\times$ slower than partitioned RMW, because partitioning had already turned the target misses into L2 hits.
\item Along the way we ended up producing the first actual evaluation of the construction prefetch RocksDB ships behind a ``TODO: verify/validate'' comment (§6). It is worth 1.5--1.7$\times$ on unsorted banding. Partitioning mostly subsumes it.
\end{enumerate}

All measurements come from an i9-14900HX (AVX2, no AVX-512). Comparative numbers are produced by the field's reference benchmark suites, run unmodified at pinned versions: fastfilter\_cpp, whose ribbon implementations are the ribbon author's own, and BuRR. The only benchmark code we wrote is a thin driver that permutes the input order around the unmodified kernel, so every measured difference is attributable to input order alone. The repository holds the raw output of every run and a script that re-derives each number in this paper from it. We replicate the ARM results on two additional microarchitectures (\S\ref{sec:arm}).

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
\textbf{Ribbon banding.} A ribbon filter solves a banded GF(2) system. Each key hashes to a start column s and a w-bit coefficient row, and insertion XOR-reduces that row against whatever is already stored until an empty slot absorbs it. The lineage matters here. Dietzfelbinger and Walzer showed that sorting rows by start position makes banded elimination linear (SGAUSS). The ribbon paper's whole pitch was that you don't need the sort — insert on the fly, in arbitrary order. BuRR then brought the sort back (ips2ra, by bucket) because bumping needs it. Homogeneous ribbon, the variant we target, never fails construction and stores no per-key result rows.

\textbf{What the filter stores and how a query works.} A ribbon filter is an $m \times r$ bit matrix Z. Hashing maps each key k to three values: a start position s(k), a w-bit coefficient row c(k) whose first bit is always 1, and r result bits b(k). The filter represents k by satisfying one linear equation over GF(2): $c(k)\cdot Z = b(k)$, with c(k) occupying columns $[s(k), s(k){+}w)$. A query recomputes s, c, and b and answers ``present'' exactly when $c\cdot Z = b$. A key that was never inserted passes that test by chance with probability $2^{-r}$, which is the false-positive rate; at r = 7 that is just under 0.8\%.

\textbf{Why banding is local.} Construction solves the system incrementally: each insertion XOR-reduces its row against stored rows until an empty slot absorbs it. Because a key's coefficients span only w consecutive columns, all of the work for that key happens in a short run of slots beginning at s(k), and the expected run is short~\citep{dillinger2021ribbon}. Every technique in this paper rests on that fact: a key belongs to the neighborhood of its start position, so grouping keys by start region confines banding's memory traffic to one region at a time, and a thread that owns a range of slots can process every key whose run provably stays inside it.

\textbf{The homogeneous variant.} Homogeneous ribbon stores no per-key result rows during banding and never fails: a row that reduces to zero is dropped, trading a small false-positive elevation for guaranteed completion. Our configuration throughout is w = 64, r = 7, and $m \approx 1.09n$ slots --- 7.63 bits per key at a measured 0.81\% false-positive rate.

\textbf{Binary fuse} construction partially sorts keys by segment explicitly to reduce cache misses~\citep{graf2022binaryfuse}; our Phase-0 characterization measures that fix directly (plain xor: 9.45 misses/key; binary fuse: 1.08).

\section{Where layout tricks cannot work}\label{sec:nulls}
Both negatives in this section are failures under favorable conditions fixed in advance --- the batched probe was measured with every address already in cache, and the read-free builder was the design its experiment was built around --- so neither is a strawman that was allowed to fail.

\subsection{Point probes are MLP-bound}
Experiment E0 compared probe strategies over identical split-block Bloom filters (Parquet geometry), 32KiB up to 512MiB, with the protocol frozen before any timing run. Our vertical, lane-structured batch probe does auto-vectorize from plain Rust — the packed-SIMD disassembly is committed. It still loses. At every size, even with every address forced L1-hot, per-key scalar probing wins (665 vs. 555 Mops/s), because a split-block check is already SIMD within a single key, and an AVX2 gather cannot beat eight independent scalar loads that the out-of-order window overlaps for free. The RAM-scale picture is starker: the identical instruction stream runs 5–6$\times$ faster when L1-hot. Probing is bound by memory-level parallelism, full stop. Prefetch was already at roughly 88\% of the single-core outstanding-miss ceiling. We measured it. There is no compute bottleneck left for a layout to remove.

\subsection{Blocked-Bloom construction is prefetch-hidable}
Experiment E1a measured five bulk builders at 1M to 429M keys, gated on bit-identical output (OR is commutative, so any deviation is a bug). Per-key insertion does degrade at scale — one random read-modify-write miss per key, 11.5 ns/key at 100M, IPC 0.48 — so there was a real cost to attack. The attacks mostly failed. Radix-partitioned construction after \citet{schmidt2021partitioned} reached only 1.45–1.71$\times$. A software-pipelined prefetch insert, the shape RocksDB ships, hit 6.1–8.9 ns/key with zero transient memory and actually beat partitioning up to 100M keys. And the variant we designed this experiment around — a read-free apply that partitions pre-merged (block, mask) pairs, OR-aggregates each block in registers, and issues exactly one store per block without ever reading filter memory — came in 1.5$\times$ slower than partitioned RMW. Non-temporal stores changed nothing. In hindsight the accounting is obvious: partitioning had already turned the expensive misses into L2 hits, so the extra grouping pass was spending real time to save cheap loads. Bloom construction misses are independent, and the memory system hides independent misses better than we could.

\section{Partition-instead-of-sort for ribbon}\label{sec:ribbon}
\subsection{The target, measured}
We first measured where ribbon construction spends its time, using the reference benchmark
suite unmodified (three repetitions). At 100 million keys, homogeneous ribbon construction
costs 50.6 ns per key --- and the hardware counters say why: the processor completes only 0.64
instructions per cycle and takes 5.81 cache misses per key, which is to say it spends most of
its time waiting on memory. The reason is structural. Each key starts its elimination at an
effectively random position in a 1.3GB table, and every reduction step needs the result of the
previous XOR before it can even compute which slot it touches next. A prefetcher cannot run
ahead of a chain like that. This is exactly the opposite of Bloom construction, where each
key's memory accesses are independent and the hardware overlaps them for free.
\subsection{Design}
We only permute the input. One counting pass buckets keys by floor(s / $2^{16}$) — windows of $2^{16}$ slots, roughly 768KiB of banding state, under half an L2 — recomputing the hash on the fly instead of materializing pairs; a second pass writes the keys back out in window order. The untouched reference kernel, prefetch pipeline and all, then consumes the permuted array. Everything downstream — banding, back-substitution, queries — stays the ribbon author's code. Figure~\ref{fig:order} shows the access-pattern change this buys.

\section{Portability across architectures}\label{sec:arm}
We repeated the construction measurements on two ARM cores: an Ampere Altra (Neoverse-N1,
NEON) and a Google Axion (Neoverse-V2, SVE), each on eight-or-fewer cores in the cloud.
Table~\ref{tab:arm} gives the 100M-key wall-clock picture. Pleating wins on all three
architectures, and by a wider margin on the ARM cores: 2.05$\times$ over the reference build on
x86, 2.37$\times$ on N1, 2.72$\times$ on V2. This is what the mechanism predicts --- the less
aggressively a core hides the dependent-chain misses, the more removing them helps --- and the
reference banding cost tracks it (46, 114, 86 ns/key on x86/N1/V2). Parallel construction
scales near-linearly on the eight-core V2: banding speeds up 1.94/3.78/7.07$\times$ at
2/4/8 threads, for a 17.3\,ns/key total.

The correctness property carries across architectures without qualification. The solved filter
is byte-for-byte identical on all three cores: at every one of the nine (size, seed) points, the
Axion fingerprint equals both the x86 and the N1 fingerprint, and every strategy on every core
agrees with the sequential build. Order-independence is thus also architecture-independence ---
the same filter, bit for bit, whether banded on AVX2, NEON, or SVE, and whether banded on one
thread or eight. Zero false negatives on every run.

\section{Order-independence and parallel construction}\label{sec:parallel}
\newtheorem{proposition}{Proposition}
\begin{proposition}[Order-independence]\label{prop:order}
Let $E=\{(r_k,b_k)\}$ be equations over $\mathrm{GF}(2)^m$ whose rows are linearly
independent, each $r_k$ carrying a leading 1 at its start position. Then banding (store each
reduced row at the slot of its leading position) followed by back-substitution with all free
slots fixed to zero yields the same solution $Z$ under every insertion order of $E$.
\end{proposition}
\emph{Proof sketch.} The occupied-slot set equals the canonical pivot set of
$V=\mathrm{span}\{r_k\}$, which depends only on $V$; the stored system is row-equivalent to
$E$; and pinning the order-independent free coordinates to zero extends $E$ to a full-rank
system with a unique solution. (Full argument in the repository,
\texttt{docs/order-independence-proof.md}; with dependent rows the enforced subset may differ
by order, which the per-run fingerprint gate detects.)

Empirically this held everywhere we looked: across every sequential and parallel run --- sizes
up to 400 million keys, six different insertion orders, thread counts up to 16 --- the finished
filter came out byte-for-byte identical, checked by hashing the output bits. The practical
consequence is larger than it first appears. If the output cannot depend on insertion order,
then any reordering scheme, including a parallel one, can prove its own correctness by
comparing one checksum against the sequential build. What is normally the hard part of
parallel data-structure construction --- arguing that no interleaving corrupts the result ---
becomes a single equality test. Our parallel version assigns disjoint window ranges (split at the same key count) to threads; keys starting in $2^{14}$ slots of a range boundary switch to a sequential tail (0.015 - 0.226\% of keys), following parallel BuRR's boundary idea. Banding scales 1.81/2.83/4.25/7.56$\times$ at 2/4/8/16 threads against its own T=1 (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}) (3.5/6.3$\times$ at 8/16 threads against the best sequential banding; the parallel path carries $\sim$20\% defer/copy overhead); what remains is the work we did not parallelize: the counting pass (5.7 ns/key) and
back-substitution (3.8 ns/key at 16 threads) are still sequential, and together they now
account for most of the 11.9 ns/key total. Both are parallelizable (counting sort trivially; back-substitution as in parallel BuRR) — future work.

\section{Construction prefetch, evaluated}\label{sec:prefetch}
RocksDB ships a software-pipelined construction prefetch behind a comment reading “TODO: verify/validate” and the reference harness enables the same pipeline above 1500 slots. Our no-prefetch control isolates it: 1.5-1.7$\times$ on unsorted banding (76.9 $\to$ 46.1 ns/key at 100M). It attacks the start-locating miss but can't touch the dependent reduction chain, which is why partitioning dominates here while the similar prefetch strategy wins for Bloom construction (§3).

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
Sorted banded elimination: \citet{dietzfelbinger2019gauss}, BuRR~\citep{dillinger2022burr}, parallel BuRR~\citep{becht2024parallelburr}. Arbitrary-order banding and production engineering: ribbon~\citep{dillinger2021ribbon}, RocksDB~\citep{rocksdbribbonblog}. Cache-conscious filter design: buffered Bloom filters~\citep{canim2010buffered}, radix-partitioned filters~\citep{schmidt2021partitioned}, binary-fuse segment sort~\citep{graf2022binaryfuse}, xor-filter buffered scatter~\citep{graf2020xor}; the generic two-phase partition-then-accumulate pattern appears as propagation blocking~\citep{beamer2017propagation} with write-combining from~\citet{wassenberg2011radix}. SIMD probe-side layout: \citet{polychroniou2014vectorized}, \citet{lang2019performance}, split-block Bloom~\citep{apache2021sbbf}. Our delta to BuRR is precise: BuRR sorts fully (ips2ra) to enable threshold-based bumping and tighter space; we show that for homogeneous ribbon a counting-pass partition captures 98\% of the sort's locality at 4$\times$ less reorder cost, with output neutrality making the transformation and its parallelization verifiable.

\section{Limitations and threats to validity}\label{sec:limits}
We characterize the miss mechanism with hardware counters on x86 only; the two ARM replications (\S\ref{sec:arm}) confirm the wall-clock speedup and the bit-identical output, but cloud ARM hypervisors do not expose the PMU, so the dependent-miss mechanism itself is not directly measured on ARM. It is, however, exactly what the ARM timing behavior implies. A bare-metal ARM run would close this, and is the one measurement we would add. We accelerate the \emph{homogeneous} ribbon ($\sim$9.5\% space overhead at r=7) in comparison to BuRR (which is sub-1\% overhead); the comparison with BuRR is an anchor, not an apples-to-apples comparison. Sort baselines use BuRR's own sequential ips2ra, but not its parallel form. Our parallel comparison is limited to banding, and its headline ratios are against the parallel path's own single-thread run. The order-independence proposition is proved for linearly independent rows, and the dependent case is handled empirically by the fingerprint gate. LSM end-to-end integration (build-during-compaction) is future work.

\section{Conclusion}
On the modern OoO x86, memory-layout optimization for filters pays in exactly one place that we could find — and it is the place that matters: the dependent-miss chains of ribbon construction, the very cost that keeps space-optimal filters out of production write paths. A one-pass partition closes 98\% of the gap the literature closes with a full sort, at a quarter of the price; the order-independence makes it safe and its parallelization checkable; and 16 threads build ribbon filters as fast as one thread inserts into a Bloom filter. The two nulls are not failures of the method but the map that located the win.
